Climate-Induced Migration, Loss and Damage, and Trafficking Vulnerability Among Haor Communities

Recurrent floods displace hundreds of thousands of people in Bangladesh’s haor wetlands, yet what happens to them afterwards, including their exposure to trafficking, has not been measured. This study follows the chain from climate loss and damage, through displacement, to trafficking vulnerability in Sunamganj district.

AuthorsNayem, J., Jaman, F., Hasan, M. M., & Tasnim, S.
Study AreaTahirpur & Bishwambarpur, Sunamganj
MethodHousehold Survey · Sentinel-2 LULC · Random Forest · Life Stories

Overview

In the haor basin, a Ramsar-listed wetland in northeastern Bangladesh, pre-monsoon flash floods arrive in April–May and destroy the Boro rice crop, the only annual harvest, in its final weeks. Months of inundation then follow. That is economic loss and damage (harvest, land, income) and non-economic loss and damage (displacement, broken kinship networks, lost farming identity and floodplain knowledge) at the same time.

The study introduces the Integrated Loss and Damage–Migration–Vulnerability (ILDMV) Framework, which separates economic from non-economic loss and damage and traces both through four tiers: climate parameters, loss and damage, migration drivers, and downstream vulnerability. It was tested with a concurrent mixed-methods design: a household survey (n = 384), three key informant interviews, 22 community life-story narratives, Sentinel-2 land-cover change analysis for 2017–2023, and machine-learning classification.

Key Results

  1. Exposure, not poverty, drives displacement. The Climate Vulnerability Index was the dominant predictor of displacement (OR = 3.21, p < 0.001), while pre-displacement income was not (OR = 1.02, p = 0.78). A Random Forest classifier agreed (AUC-ROC = 0.87), with the index ranked first among 12 features. Of 384 households, 51.8% reported climate-induced displacement.
  2. Productive land is disappearing in clusters. Cultivable agricultural land declined by 12.3% between 2017 and 2023 (land-cover overall accuracy 88.4%, Kappa 0.856), and the loss clustered around haor perimeters (Moran’s I = 0.62, p < 0.001).
  3. Displacement deepens the damage. Mean monthly income fell by 39.3% after displacement, extreme poverty nearly doubled (22.9% to 44.8%), and primary-sector work dropped from 52.6% to 18.4% as informal daily labour rose from 8.2% to 31.4%.
  4. Resilience did not prevent displacement. Household resilience was not a significant predictor, which supports treating these cases as an adaptation limit under the UNFCCC Loss and Damage framework. It is better read as a recovery asset than a protection.
  5. Gender-based violence rose, mostly for women. Displaced households reported more physical violence than non-displaced ones (14.8% vs 6.2%), and displaced women were more than three times as likely as displaced men to experience it (OR = 3.14).
  6. Trafficking vulnerability is substantial. 38.7% of displaced respondents scored High on a new Trafficking Vulnerability Indicator built on ILO and UNODC criteria. Life stories pointed to three pathways: deceptive recruitment, debt bondage, and protective child marriage.

Policy Relevance

The findings support Loss and Damage finance that compensates both economic and non-economic harm, social protection and migration governance that reach displaced households, and anti-trafficking work aimed at both the financial-desperation and the social-isolation pathways.
